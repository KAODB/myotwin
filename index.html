Countermeasures and their evidence

    What if

    Change an assumption and the simulated nights are regenerated.

    How it works

    An assumed sensor panel in the sleep station. Nothing is worn. The one requirement is bare calves on the sensor strip.

    Impedance strip under the calves

    Dry electrodes in the mattress pass a tiny 50 kHz current through the calf. In people with a leg in a cast, this reading was 28% lower than in the other leg and recovered after the cast came off (measured on the shin). We found no published measurement in space or through a mattress: that is what this panel is for.

    60 GHz radar above the bunk

    A low-power radar reads chest movement from a distance: breathing rate and heart rate. Sleep time is estimated from movement and breathing, which is an assumption here. No contact, no camera. Radar reads breathing and heart rate on Earth; we found no test in space.

    Particle trackers above and below

    Tracker planes record the path of each cosmic-ray particle crossing the sleeper. That gives the night's dose and the direction it came from. Summed over many tracks, the small deflections also give a coarse density image of the body with no added dose.

    Where the method comes from

    On Earth, clinics look for muscle loss in older adults in three steps. MyoTwin takes those steps to a bunk on the Moon or Mars.

    StepIn a clinic on EarthIn a bunk on the Moon or Mars
    FindA tape around the calf, or a five-question form. V95The calf is read by impedance every night. Phase angle from bioimpedance is lower in people with muscle loss. V96
    AssessA grip test, or five rises from a chair. V94 V95A strength test on the exercise device, asked for when the nightly reading falls behind the mission plan. V82
    ConfirmMuscle mass by DXA or bioimpedance. V94 V95The flight surgeon, with the impedance trend and the density image.

    The Canadian Space Agency describes the effects seen in astronauts as similar to accelerated aging and health problems caused by a sedentary lifestyle. V47 The clinic tools read the whole body or the hand. Reading one calf by impedance in a bunk is this project's adaptation of them.

    Does partial gravity protect muscle? The model against NASA open data

    The muscle model assumes how much of the weightlessness loss remains at partial gravity. NASA's Open Science Data Repository holds a related test in rats: 40% weight bearing in a harness, an analog of partial gravity.

    What this version does not account for, and the next step

    1. Not accounted for: The momentum of each cosmic-ray particle. Deflection depends on momentum as much as on thickness, and momenta span orders of magnitude.

      Next step: Measure a reference block beside the sleeper every night, then add a layer that measures momentum.

    2. Not accounted for: Movement during the night and the tails from nuclear interactions. We expect that the statistics support trends over large regions and several weeks, not small features (expected, not computed).

      Next step: Sort tracks by posture using the pressure map, and run a particle-transport simulation.

    3. Not accounted for: Habitat shielding changes which particles arrive, and thick body regions filter out low-momentum particles, so scattering does not scale linearly with thickness. One reference block calibrates one thickness only.

      Next step: Simulate the tracks behind the habitat and through the body.

    4. Not accounted for: Scattering measures all the material along each path. Bedding, body fluid, fat and muscle all add to it, and water and muscle differ by under 3% in radiation length, so fluid loss and muscle loss look alike.

      Next step: Compare the signal summed over the whole limb, with the bedding fixed or measured, and read it beside the impedance.

    5. Not accounted for: Any real density image of a body made this way: we found none published.

      Next step: Repeat the published ground test with sea-level muons and a body phantom.

    6. Not accounted for: Human impedance data from space, from bed rest or from the back of the calf, and any study of bed-integrated dry electrodes: we found none published.

      Next step: Run the panel beside standard strength tests and MRI in a bed-rest study.

    7. Not accounted for: The impedance baseline is taken on Earth, where the calf presses on the strip about six times harder than on the Moon (2.6 times harder than on Mars), and fluid shifts move the reading. The size of the step on arrival is our assumption.

      Next step: Measure in the last hour before waking, re-baseline in the first surface week, and measure the arrival step in a partial-gravity analogue.

    8. Not accounted for: Radar vital signs in space: we found no published test.

      Next step: Test in a mock sleep station and in an analogue.

    9. Not accounted for: Radiation. The card shows absorbed dose (in silicon on Mars, water-corrected at the Moon), not effective dose, and neutrons are not measured. The Moon nights replay a lightly shielded detector on an orbiter; behind thick habitat shielding a crew would receive much less from a solar particle event.

      Next step: Run the measured proton spectrum through a validated transport model of the habitat, and add a neutron channel.

    10. Not accounted for: Detection accuracy. The simulated nights and the plan come from the same model, so this page shows the workflow, not how well a real decline would be caught.

      Next step: Test the rules on recorded bed-rest data.

    11. Not accounted for: The transit. In weightlessness the sleeper does not rest on the panel, so MyoTwin starts at arrival.

      Next step: Pair it with a worn sensor for the transit.

    12. Not accounted for: Mood, air, noise and light are not read.

      Next step: Show the habitat's environmental sensors beside the brief.
