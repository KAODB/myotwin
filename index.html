      How it works

      An assumed sensor panel in the sleep stationbed. Nothing is worn. The one requirement is bare calves on the sensor strip.

      Impedance strip under the calves

      Dry electrodes in the mattress pass a tiny 50 kHz current through the calf. In people with a leg in a cast, this reading was 28% lower than in the other leg and recovered after the cast came off (measured on the shin). We found no published measurement in space or through a mattress: that is what this panel is for.

      60 GHz radar above the bunkbed

      A low-power radar reads chest movement from a distance: breathing rate and heart rate. Sleep time is estimated from movement and breathing, which is an assumption here. No contact, no camera. Radar reads breathing and heart rate on Earth; we found no test in space.

      Particle trackers above and below

      Tracker planes record the path of each cosmic-ray particle crossing the sleeper. That gives the night's dose and the direction it came from. Summed over many tracks, the small deflections also give a coarse density image of the body with no added dose. This image is experimental.

      What this version does not account for, and the next step

      1. Not accounted for: The momentum of each particle. Deflection depends on momentum as much as on thickness, and momenta span orders of magnitude.

        Next step: Measure a block of known thickness beside the sleeper every night and use the ratio; then assess a momentum-measuring layer.

      2. Not accounted for: Movement during the night and the long tails from nuclear interactions. We expect that the statistics support trends over large regions and several weeks, not small features (expected, not computed).

        Next step: Sort tracks by posture using the pressure image, start with calf, thigh and trunk, and run a particle-transport simulation.

      3. Not accounted for: Habitat shielding. Shielding removes low-energy particles and adds secondaries. We expect that the best-protected sleep stations give the weakest image (expected, not computed).

        Next step: Simulate the usable tracks behind different shielding and find the habitats where the method works.

      4. Not accounted for: Any real measurement of the density image.

        Next step: Repeat the published ground test with sea-level muons and a body phantom before proposing a flight experiment.

      5. Not accounted for: Human impedance data in space, in bed rest, or at the back of the calf, and any test of bed-integrated dry electrodes.

        Next step: Run the panel beside standard strength tests and MRI in a bed-rest study and set the thresholds from the measured night-to-night noise.

      6. Not accounted for: Fluid shifts in partial gravity, which move the impedance reading.

        Next step: Fix the measurement to the last hour before waking and characterise the arrival step.

      7. Not accounted for: Radar vital signs in space.

        Next step: Test in a mock sleep station and in an analogue.

      8. Not accounted for: Detection accuracy. The simulated nights and the plan come from the same model, so this page shows the workflow, not how well a real decline would be caught.

        Next step: Test the rules on recorded bed-rest data.

      9. Not accounted for: The transit. In weightlessness the sleeper does not rest on the panel, so MyoTwin starts at arrival.

        Next step: Pair it with a worn sensor for the transit.

      10. Not accounted for: Effective dose. The radiation card shows absorbed dose: in silicon on Mars, water-corrected by the instrument team on the Moon.

        Next step: Add the conversion with a validated transport model.

      All data here are simulated except the replayed radiation measurements, the sensor panel has not been built, and the status rules are interim and unvalidated.
